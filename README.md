## Motivation

An AI system asked to make a scientific discovery does not begin from a blank state. It
arrives with concepts, associations, and theories acquired during pretraining,
fine-tuning, or earlier interactions. Those priors can be useful: they may let the system
recognize a pattern from very little data. But they may also make a familiar explanation
harder to abandon when new evidence points elsewhere.

Most law-discovery evaluations score only the final answer:

> Did the model recover the correct equation?

That leaves out an important part of discovery:

> What did the model believe before seeing the evidence, and how did that belief affect
> when and how it revised its conclusion?

Two systems can eventually reach the same law while following different paths. One may
revise as soon as the first anomaly appears; another may preserve parts of its prior until
the contradiction is replicated or a more diagnostic experiment is observed.

This repository is a first step toward benchmarks that measure that **revision
trajectory**, not only endpoint accuracy.

## Our angle

The project does not attempt to reproduce open-ended scientific discovery. Instead, it
constructs a small, controlled diagnostic in which three ingredients can be separated:

1. **Prior knowledge:** a fictional law supplied in context or learned through LoRA.
2. **New evidence:** numerical observations generated by a different law.
3. **Belief revision:** the law selected after each batch of evidence.

Conceptually, the benchmark studies

$$\text{stored prior} + \text{sequential evidence} \longrightarrow \text{revision trajectory}.$$

The scientific domain is fictional, reducing the chance that the particular mapping
between variable names and equations appeared in pretraining. The observations use
controlled one-variable-at-a-time interventions, so each exponent can be inferred from
numerical ratios without requiring an external calculator.

This creates a deliberately simple “wind tunnel” for studying a difficult question:

> Can a model benefit from stored knowledge without losing the ability to revise that
> knowledge when the observations disagree?

## The task

The model observes four fictional quantities—$m$, $r$, $q$, and a distractor $s$—and
must infer a power law for $\tau$.

The prior law is

$$L_A:\qquad \tau = 3\frac{mr^2}{q},$$

while the new observations are generated by

$$L_B:\qquad \tau = 3\frac{mr}{q^2}.$$

The two laws agree on the constant and on the exponent of $m$. They disagree only on the
exponents of $r$ and $q$. The competing law $L_B$ is never named in the prompt: the model
must infer it from the measurements.

This narrow construction lets us distinguish three possibilities:

- the model follows the data;
- the model repeats its prior despite the data;
- the model revises only part of the prior and temporarily proposes an intermediate law.

## Experimental story

### 1. Establish that the model can infer the law

Before studying prior bias, the untouched Qwen3-8B model was tested on controlled
power-law induction with no competing prior.

Across 20 independently generated datasets and five samples per dataset:

- 96 of 100 generations returned the exact law in the requested JSON schema;
- the remaining four traces also derived the correct law but either violated the output
  contract or reached the token limit;
- no parsed response proposed a scientifically incorrect law.

This feasibility gate matters. Without it, failure under a prior could simply mean that
the model could not solve the numerical task in the first place.

### 2. Supply the prior in context

The first comparison asked whether merely stating $L_A$ as background knowledge would
prevent the model from recovering $L_B$ from a complete, clean evidence table.

Ten matched datasets were evaluated under three prompt arms:

| Prompt arm | Exact valid $L_B$ outputs | Scientifically correct traces | Returned $L_A$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| No prior | 9/10 | 10/10 | 0/10 |
| Guided $L_A$ prior | 10/10 | 10/10 | 0/10 |
| Bare $L_A$ prior | 9/10 completed | 10/10 | 0/10 |

The guided arm explicitly allowed the observations to contradict the prior. The bare arm
simply described $L_A$ as established knowledge, removing that possible cue. Its one
incomplete response had already derived exact $L_B$ before reaching the token limit.

**Finding:** when the full contradiction was clean and decisive, an in-context prior
produced no detectable bias toward $L_A$.

That negative result motivated the second experiment. If a complete evidence table
immediately overwhelms the prior, the interaction must be examined earlier in the
revision process.

### 3. Encode the prior in the model weights

An 80-step LoRA adapter was trained to teach Qwen3-8B the fictional law $L_A$. The
adapter retrieved $L_A$ before seeing evidence in all three preregistration runs, while
the untouched base model reported no prior.

This was a real but incomplete prior. It generalized to 3 of 6 held-out recall prompts
with thinking enabled and 0 of 6 with thinking disabled. It is therefore described here
as a **retrievable, prompt-dependent weight prior**, not as fully portable knowledge.

### 4. Reveal contradictory evidence sequentially

Rather than presenting all of $L_B$ at once, observations were revealed in stages:

| Stage | Evidence revealed | What it diagnoses |
|---:|---|---|
| 0 | No observations | The model’s preregistered belief |
| 1 | Baseline and $m$ interventions | Evidence compatible with both laws |
| 2 | First anomalous $r$ intervention | Initial contradiction of $r^2$ |
| 3 | Replicated $r$ evidence and first $q$ intervention | Stronger, multi-variable conflict |
| 4 | Remaining $q$ and distractor interventions | Complete evidence for $L_B$ |

Neither candidate law was named during this protocol, and the model was not instructed
to reject its prior. Base and LoRA models received the same evidence sequence.

## Main result

| Dataset seed | Base prior | LoRA prior | Base first selects $L_B$ | LoRA first selects $L_B$ |
|---:|---|---|---:|---:|
| 7100 | none | $L_A$ | Stage 3 | Stage 3 |
| 7200 | none | $L_A$ | Stage 3 | Stage 3 |
| 7300 | none | $L_A$ | Stage 3 | Stage 4 |

Both models ultimately recovered $L_B$ in every paired run. The learned prior therefore
did **not** block discovery.

Its effect was subtler. After the first contradiction, the LoRA model revised the
$r$ exponent while retaining the still-untested $q^{-1}$ component of its prior,
temporarily proposing

$$\tau = 3\frac{mr}{q}.$$

Once $q$ was intervened on, the model usually completed the transition to $L_B$. In one
of three runs—the one with the weakest first $q$ contradiction—it required one additional
evidence batch.

> **Headline:** the weight-encoded prior remained defeasible. It did not change the final
> discovery, but it sometimes influenced the path and timing of belief revision.

Calling this “delayed reasoning” would be too strong. The benchmark observes when the
model changes its stated scientific conclusion; it does not directly measure the speed or
quality of its hidden reasoning process. The supported description is **delayed belief
revision** or **delayed law selection**.

## Why the trajectory matters

If the evaluation recorded only final accuracy, both arms would score 3/3 and appear
identical. The sequential protocol exposes information that endpoint scoring discards:

- whether a prior is present before evidence;
- which components of that prior survive an initial anomaly;
- whether revision is incremental or abrupt;
- how much replicated or discriminating evidence is required before the selected law
  changes.

The current result is a proof of concept, not a population estimate. Its contribution is
the experimental framing: prior knowledge can be manipulated, evidence can be held fixed,
and revision can be measured stage by stage.

## What the project does—and does not—show

The checked-in evidence supports the following:

- the base model can infer the controlled power laws from numerical observations;
- a contextual prior is overridden by complete, decisive evidence;
- the selected LoRA creates a retrievable but incomplete $L_A$ prior;
- that prior survives initially compatible evidence and can shape intermediate
  hypotheses;
- both base and prior-bearing models ultimately recover the conflicting law;
- one of three prior-bearing runs revises one evidence batch later than its matched base
  run.

It does **not** establish that:

- prior knowledge generally delays scientific discovery;
- the effect scales across models, laws, or prior strengths;
- the model has performed open-ended scientific discovery;
- knowledge and reasoning occupy mechanistically separate parts of the network;
- the observed trajectory would persist under noisy or ambiguous data.

Important current limitations are one model family, one pair of laws, one selected LoRA
checkpoint, three sequential paired runs, and an intentionally restricted power-law
hypothesis class.

## Where this goes next

This pilot isolates one law pair, one selected adapter, and a small number of evidence
trajectories. Several extensions would turn the diagnostic into a broader discovery
benchmark.

### Counterbalance the scientific laws

The current experiment always teaches $L_A$ and generates new evidence from $L_B$. A
larger benchmark should contain many matched law pairs and reverse which law acts as the
prior. This would separate genuine resistance to revision from a model’s pre-existing
preference for particular variables, integer exponents, or equation shapes.

The hypothesis class could then expand from multiplicative power laws to additive,
exponential, rational, piecewise, and conservation-law relationships.

### Measure prior strength rather than treating it as binary

A law is not simply known or unknown. Future experiments should create a graded family
of priors through different fine-tuning durations, curriculum sizes, adapter scales, or
memory-retrieval strengths.

Prior strength should also be measured behaviorally—for example, through held-out recall
and the probability assigned to competing laws—rather than inferred only from training
hyperparameters. This would allow a switching curve of the form

$$P(\widehat{L}=L_B)=F(\text{prior strength},\text{evidence strength}).$$

### Map the evidence threshold for revision

The present sequential protocol provides only four evidence stages. A denser design could
vary:

- the number of observations;
- the size of the disagreement between $L_A$ and $L_B$;
- whether contradictions are replicated;
- which variable is intervened on first;
- whether the evidence attacks one component or the entire prior.

The goal would be to estimate how much diagnostic evidence is required before a system
switches theories, and whether the transition is smooth, abrupt, or componentwise.

### Introduce noise, ambiguity, and spurious correlations

Clean evidence was useful for establishing the basic phenomenon, but real scientific data
are rarely decisive. Future versions should test:

- measurement noise;
- too few observations to identify a unique law;
- distractor variables correlated only in the observed sample;
- outliers and contradictory measurements;
- two theories with nearly indistinguishable predictions.

These regimes would reveal whether prior knowledge improves sample efficiency when it is
correct while creating inertia when it is wrong.

### Compare different ways of storing knowledge

The current comparison covers an in-context statement and one LoRA adapter. The same
protocol could compare:

- full-parameter fine-tuning;
- other parameter-efficient adapters;
- retrieval-augmented memory;
- editable knowledge graphs;
- recurrent or continual-learning state;
- explicit symbolic rules.

This would test whether different memory mechanisms produce different forms of
stickiness, locality, and reversibility.

### Move from passive inference to active discovery

Here the benchmark chooses the observations and the model only interprets them. A more
capable scientific agent should decide what to measure next. The model could select an
intervention that maximally distinguishes its current prior from an alternative, where
$\mathrm{IG}$ denotes expected information gain:

$$x_{t+1}=\arg\max_x \mathrm{IG}(x\mid\mathcal{H}_t).$$

This would let the benchmark ask whether a strong prior merely biases interpretation or
also discourages the model from running the experiment most likely to falsify it.

### Connect the synthetic benchmark to historical discovery

The long-term motivation is a controlled “rewind” question: if direct access to a
scientific result were removed or suppressed, could a model reconstruct it from the
observations and tools available before the discovery?

Synthetic laws are a necessary first step because the prior, evidence, and correct answer
can be controlled exactly. A historically grounded extension would additionally need to
control indirect leakage through terminology, downstream consequences, derivations, and
related facts. The present benchmark supplies the behavioral template for that harder
problem:

$$\text{prior scientific state} + \text{historically available evidence} \longrightarrow \text{discovery or resistance}.$$

Together, these directions would shift evaluation from asking only whether a model can
produce a correct theory to asking how its accumulated knowledge changes what it notices,
which experiments it chooses, and how much evidence it requires to change its mind.
